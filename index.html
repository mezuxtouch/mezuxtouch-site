GPR Survey Services: A Buyer's Guide for Utility Projects

How to choose and order a ground penetrating radar survey before digging, drilling or laying cable.

Ordering GPR survey services for the first time can be confusing. Quotes use different units, providers offer different outputs, and it is not always clear which survey type a project actually needs. This guide explains what a ground penetrating radar survey does, the main survey types used on utility projects in India, what happens on site, what data you should expect and how pricing is usually set.

What a GPR survey does

Ground penetrating radar sends short electromagnetic pulses into the ground from an antenna moved across the surface. When a pulse meets a buried object made of a different material — a metal cable, a plastic duct, a concrete chamber, a water pipe — part of the signal reflects back. The operator reads these reflections to work out the location and approximate depth of each service. Unlike older single-frequency locators, GPR can detect non-metallic features such as PVC and HDPE ducts, which makes it useful on mixed utility corridors.

Where GPR survey services are used

Most requests come from projects that are about to disturb the ground in a place where other services already run. Typical examples include:

  • Construction sites — before foundations, pits or basements are excavated next to existing roads and buildings.
  • HDD road and rail crossings — where the drill passes under a corridor that may already hold cables and pipes.
  • Telecom and power cable routes — before new ducts or cables are laid along a road.
  • Water, sewer and gas works — when new lines must be placed between existing ones.
  • Record updating — when an owner wants accurate utility mapping of an area whose old drawings are incomplete.

GPR compared with other detection methods

GPR is one of several subsurface investigation methods, and each has a place. Electromagnetic cable locators work well on metallic cables that can carry a signal, but they cannot see plastic pipes or empty ducts. Trial pits give certainty at one point, but they are slow, disruptive and only show what lies in that one hole. Radar technology fills the gap between the two: its waves pass through soil and reflect from objects of any material, so it can scan long stretches quickly and show where to dig a trial pit if confirmation is needed. On busy corridors, the best results usually come from combining GPR scanning with site observation and targeted trial pits.

What happens on site

  1. Scope check — the route or area, access, traffic conditions and the outputs you need are agreed first.
  2. Grid or line scanning — the GPR unit is moved along planned scan lines across and along the corridor.
  3. On-site marking — detected services are marked on the ground for the work crew.
  4. Data processing — scans are reviewed to confirm alignments and approximate depths.
  5. Reporting — a drawing, and an L-section where required, is prepared for the design and execution teams.

Preparing for the survey day

A little preparation helps the team cover more ground with better results. Share the planned route or work area on a drawing or map, along with any old utility records you already have. Arrange access to the site and, on roads, any traffic management that is needed. Clear loose material, parked vehicles or stacked equipment from the scan lines where possible, because the antenna needs to move steadily over the surface. Finally, agree in advance who will receive the drawing and in what format, so the survey data reaches the design and construction teams without delay.

Factors that affect results

  • Soil and moisture — dry or sandy soil allows deeper penetration; wet clay reduces it.
  • Surface type — reinforced concrete and metal covers can mask what lies beneath.
  • Service size and material — small plastic pipes at depth give weaker reflections than metal cables.
  • Congestion — many services close together need careful interpretation and, sometimes, trial pits to confirm.

What to ask a GPR survey provider

  • Which outputs are included — ground marking, drawing, L-section — and in which format?
  • How are areas with poor detection reported, and are trial pits recommended?
  • Is the rate per metre of route, and is there a minimum order?
  • Can the same team support follow-on HDD or cable laying work?
